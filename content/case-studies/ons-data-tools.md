## The problem: writing without knowing who you're writing for

ONS statistical content is written by statisticians — highly expert in their subject, but typically without a background in digital content or analytics. When I arrived, most publishing teams had no way of knowing how their pages were performing. Pageviews, bounce rates, and search terms were locked inside a central analytics function that teams had to request access to through a formal process.

The result: teams made content decisions based on intuition and tradition rather than evidence. Pages that nobody read were maintained at the same cost as high-traffic pages. Search terms that signalled user confusion went unnoticed.

The style guide situation was equally fragmented. Style.ONS — the ONS house style guide — existed but hadn't been overhauled in years. There was no process for proposing or evidencing style changes. Different teams had developed their own local conventions.

---

## Self-service analytics: a dashboard for every team

I built a self-service analytics dashboard in Google Data Studio, giving every ONS publishing team direct access to data about their own pages — without needing to request it from a central team or know how to use Google Analytics directly.

The dashboard combined standard metrics (pageviews, bounce rate, session duration) with content-specific signals that were more actionable for teams without an analytics background:

- Top search terms for each section
- Most-clicked headings (indicating what users were scanning for)
- Entry and exit pages

<!-- IMAGE: Google Data Studio dashboard (slide 27) -->

The dashboard was designed to be used by statisticians, not analysts. Every metric was labelled in plain English, with a short explanation of what it meant and what action it might suggest. The aim was to reduce the gap between data and decision — not just give teams numbers, but help them know what to do with them.

## Style.ONS: evidence-based governance for the house style guide

Style.ONS is the ONS house style guide — the reference document that sets standards for language, terminology, and formatting across all ONS publications. I led a full overhaul of the guide, working through it section by section to update outdated conventions, fill gaps, and bring it in line with the bulletin redesign work.

But the more important change was structural: I established a formal governance process for how style decisions were made going forward. Previously, style changes were made informally by whoever had the access and inclination. The new process required any proposed change to be accompanied by user research evidence or a clear rationale, reviewed by a small editorial panel, and communicated to all teams before taking effect.

## Internal comms: making change stick

Rolling out both a new analytics tool and a revised style guide to over 100 teams required more than sending an email. I ran an internal comms campaign that spanned posters in shared spaces across ONS offices, a newsletter series targeted at team leads, and drop-in sessions where teams could ask questions and try the dashboard with their own data.

The approach was adapted from the bulletin redesign rollout — which had taught me that statisticians adopt new ways of working when they understand the rationale, not just the rule. Both campaigns led with the "why": here's what we learned from users, here's what wasn't working, here's what changes and here's why it's better.
