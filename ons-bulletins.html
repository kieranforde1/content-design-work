Redesigning statistical bulletins.

ONS bulletins were long, structured around data sources, and not built for the people reading them. I ran the research to prove it, redesigned the format, and helped 100+ publishing teams adopt a new approach.

The problem

Bulletins built for producers, not readers.

ONS statistical bulletins are the primary vehicle for publishing official UK statistics — from unemployment to house prices to population figures. When I joined the team, they were long, dense, and structured around the way ONS collected data rather than the way users wanted to consume it.

A bulletin on the UK labour market might open with a 400-word preamble about methodology before revealing any headline figure. Topics that were logically connected — employment rate and unemployment rate, for example — were separated into different sections because they came from different datasets.

The content structure had been inherited from printed reports. Nobody had ever asked whether it served readers.

Discovery

Understanding who reads bulletins and why.

I led a full Discovery phase to understand how people actually used bulletins — running a site-wide survey, 14 in-depth user interviews, analytics reviews, heatmapping, and on-page polls across multiple bulletins.

Research methods

Site-wide survey (n=large), 14 user interviews across journalist, researcher, and public-sector roles, Google Analytics review, Hotjar heatmapping, and on-page polls deployed on live bulletins.

Key findings

Users came to bulletins with one of three tasks: read the analysis, download the data, or understand the methodology. Almost nobody arrived wanting to read a bulletin cover-to-cover. Navigation between sections was the primary need — not linear reading.

Users consistently said they wanted bulletins organised by topic — not by data source. A headline figure about wages should sit next to the analysis about wages, not in a separate chapter because the wages data came from a different ONS survey than the employment data.

The research also surfaced a sharp disconnect: ONS publishing teams thought of bulletins as statistical reports. Users thought of them as news articles with supporting evidence. Both were valid framings — but the existing format served neither well.

Design

A new structure, built around user tasks.

The findings pointed in one direction. I redesigned the bulletin format around the three tasks users actually came to complete:

  1. Analysis first — the headline figures and narrative, written for a general audience
  2. Data second — downloads and interactive charts, clearly signposted
  3. Methodology last — background for specialists, not buried at the start

Within each section, content was organised by topic — not by dataset or survey. Related figures from different data sources were brought together.

Image: Bulletin structure guidance

The new bulletin structure guidance table showing the recommended section order and content types for each zone, developed for distribution to publishing teams. (Portfolio slide 10)

I also introduced a new content pattern: a warning callout for provisional or revised figures. Previously, caveats about data quality were buried in methodology sections. Users — particularly journalists working on deadline — frequently missed them and published incorrect figures. The new pattern surfaced caveats inline, adjacent to the figure they referred to.

Image: Warning content pattern

The new warning callout component, shown in context within a bulletin. Used to flag provisional, revised, or methodologically caveated figures inline. (Portfolio slide 11)

Rollout

Embedding change across 100+ teams.

Redesigning the format was only half the challenge. ONS has over 100 publishing teams — each producing bulletins independently, led by statisticians without a design or content background. Getting them to adopt a new format required more than updated guidance.

I also overhauled Style.ONS (the ONS house style guide) and established an evidence-based governance process for style decisions, so future changes could be proposed, tested, and adopted systematically rather than by editorial fiat.

The internal rollout included a comms campaign — posters, a newsletter series, and team workshops — to explain the rationale behind each change and give teams confidence to apply the new format to their specific subject areas.

"I used to think the way we structured bulletins was just how statistics had to be written. Now I realise we'd been writing for ourselves, not our users."

All 100+ ONS publishing teams adopted the new format. The gender pay gap bulletin was among the first — and teams across ONS started using it as a reference for their own restructures.
