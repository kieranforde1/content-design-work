Discovery

Understanding why term documentation wasn't happening.

The terminology problem wasn't just about missing documentation — it was about why teams weren't producing it. I ran discovery interviews with 8 people across 6 product lines to understand the barriers.

What emerged: teams wanted to document their terminology but had no lightweight process for doing so. The existing approach required too much individual effort, produced inconsistent outputs, and had no clear home in the product team workflow. The process relied on content designers being proactively in the right conversations at the right time — which, at HubSpot's scale, was impossible to sustain.

The insight that shaped the governance design: teams needed a structured, time-boxed activity they could run themselves, with a clear output format that matched how the content style platform expected term information.

Workshop design

A self-serve toolkit teams could run without a content designer.

I designed a self-serve terminology workshop — a structured 60-minute session that walked product teams through identifying, defining, and preparing terms for documentation. The toolkit included a facilitation guide, a term capture template, and guidance on which terms to prioritise for the content style platform versus which to escalate to the central team.

The design principle was self-service: any PM, designer, or engineer could run the workshop using the toolkit, without needing a content designer present. This was the only approach that could scale across HubSpot's 40+ product teams.

I piloted the workshop with three teams. Across those sessions, the teams identified 22 new terms — terms that had never been formally documented and were causing inconsistency across their product areas. Each session ran in under 60 minutes.

Scaling and buy-in

Leadership buy-in and a 2025 rollout plan.

Late 2024
Scaling and leadership buy-in
Presented to design leadership. Ran 3 pilots of the self-serve workshop, where teams identified 27 terms in a single 60-minute session. Secured Director+ buy-in for a 2025 rollout, incorporating terminology into teams' quarterly planning.

Presenting to design leadership was a turning point. The combination of customer evidence (see the Terminology overview) and the workshop pilot results — 27 terms identified in a single session — made the case for treating this as a strategic priority rather than a maintenance task.

Director+ buy-in unlocked the ability to incorporate terminology workshops into teams' quarterly planning, rather than relying on individual content designers to pitch individual teams.

Code Orange

Governance at scale across three product groups.

In mid-2025, a company-wide quality initiative called Code Orange created a window to push the terminology governance work at scale. Rather than continuing to roll out workshops team by team, I pivoted to running direct analysis across product lines — using the prototype checker (Strand 2) to scan live product text and generate targeted reports.

I sent 22 reports to product owners across Automation, Data Hub, and Reporting. Each report identified specific terminology issues in their product area, categorised by severity: terms used incorrectly, terms that needed documentation, and terms that required a decision about the preferred usage.

501 of those issues were resolved by teams acting on the reports alone — no automated tooling, no follow-up chase. That's a 13.2% resolution rate from a manual process. It's meaningful evidence that teams act on terminology issues when they're surfaced clearly. The remaining 4,608 are the target for the engineering rebuild.

The sprint demonstrated that the governance model could operate at a scale that was impossible when relying solely on workshops — and provided the behavioural proof needed to make the case for automation.

Director+ buy-in. Terminology governance incorporated into teams' quarterly planning — shifting it from a reactive, ad-hoc activity to a planned, recurring one.

The Code Orange reports. 22 reports to product owners, identifying 5,311 issues across Automation, Data Hub, and Reporting. 501 were resolved by teams acting on the reports alone — a 13.2% fix rate with no automation. The remaining 4,608 are the target for the engineering rebuild.

The foundation for automation. The governance work established the documentation standards and team buy-in that make the engineering rebuild viable. The rules layer of the checker depends on well-documented terms. Without the governance strand, Strand 3 would have nothing to check against.
